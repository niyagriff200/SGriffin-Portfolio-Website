Introduction

            The final dance between Harrison and the ballerina is probably one of the most memorable moments in Kurt Vonnegut’s 1961 short story Harrison Bergeron. For a brief moment, both characters are free from the handicaps imposed on them and show what humanity can do when individual talent is allowed to flourish. Before I explain why I chose to depict this scene, it helps to understand the world it takes place in.

In Harrison Bergeron, the United States has been transformed into a society where everyone is forced to be equal. No one is allowed to be more intelligent, athletic, or even beautiful than another. Anyone who exceeds these standards is forced to wear heavy handicaps or masks to suppress their abilities. Harrison, the title character, refuses to accept those restrictions, and after escaping government custody, he interrupts a live broadcast, declaring himself Emperor and choosing a ballerina to be his Empress. He removes their handicaps, and together they perform an extraordinary dance before the Handicapper General shoots and kills them. It is that brief and powerful moment that inspired my image, The Last Dance.

Using Molly Bang’s principles of color, shape, placement, size to represent the conflict between individuality and oppression, I wanted to create an image that illustrated both the triumph of Harrison and the ballerina’s brief moment of freedom and the tragedy of their deaths.

Creating The Last Dance

            In this section, I will explain why I created the image the way I did and how it connects to the short story Harrison Bergeron and the principles presented in Picture This. Every major element of the image was intentionally chosen to represent a specific aspect of the story. From the colors and shapes to even the placement of each object, the following sections will discuss these choices and the thought process behind them.  

Choosing the Scene

            First things first, why did I choose the scene that I did? To me, the dance scene was the climax of the story. More importantly, it represented the complete opposite of what the society was designed to enforce. Throughout the story, we see how people are forced to hide their talents so no one can be considered better than anyone else.

During the dance, however, Harrison and the ballerina removed those handicaps and embraced the very qualities the government tried to suppress. They were free to be themselves, which made the scene inspiring and ultimately tragic to me. And because the moment captured the story’s central conflict between individuality and oppression, I felt it was the best scene to represent in my image.

Colors

Purple

When choosing the colors for my image, I wanted each one to represent a different aspect of the story. I started with purple because it is often associated with royalty. Since Harrison declares, “I am the Emperor!” (Vonnegut, 1961) and chooses a ballerina to become his Empress, I felt purple was the most appropriate color choice to represent them. Both Harrison and the ballerina share the same color because, in the moment of the dance, they were standing united against the government’s restrictions.

This also connects to one of Molly Bang’s principles: “We associate the same or similar colors much more strongly than we associate same or similar shapes” (Bang, 2000, pp. 76-80). By making both figures purple, I wanted viewers to immediately recognize them as connected and working towards the same goal.

Black

            Black was the next color I chose for my image. In the story, the government controls every aspect of people’s lives by forcing handicaps on anyone who stands out. Because of this, I thought black was the best color to use to represent the government and its power. To me, black also represents the oppression created by the government and the way it limits individuality throughout the story.

Pale Blue

            I chose pale blue to represent not only the people that Harrison and the ballerina potentially inspired during their final dance, but also the freedom and possibilities if the people were allowed to express their individuality. Although that inspiration was short-lived because of the government’s continued enforcement of handicaps, I believe some of those who were watching the dance may have questioned the regime after witnessing the display of talent and freedom.

Red

Last but not least, the final color I chose was red. Unlike the other colors, red does not represent a person. Instead, it represents the approaching danger that Harrison and the ballerina were about to face, a danger that they did not overcome. I thought that red was the most appropriate choice because it’s commonly associated with danger, violence, and blood, all of which are present during the scene’s final moments.

Shapes

Rectangles

            There are only two rectangles in my image, and they represent Harrison and the ballerina. Why? Based on one of Molly Bang’s principles, “Vertical shapes are more exciting and more active. Vertical shapes rebel against the earth’s gravity. They imply energy and reaching towards the heights or heavens” (Bang, 2000, pp. 44-46). I felt this described Harrison and the ballerina perfectly during their dance. In fact, the story describes their dance this way: “Not only were the laws of the land abandoned, but the law of gravity and the laws of motion as well” (Vonnegut, 1961).

I also made Harrison’s rectangle taller than the ballerina’s because even though both characters are important to the scene, Harrison is the central figure in the story and the one who initiates the rebellion. In my opinion, making his rectangle taller helped emphasize his role while still showing the connection between the two characters.

Triangles

            After deciding how to represent Harrison and the ballerina, I needed a way to represent the government and the danger it posed, along with the bullets that were about to end the lives of Harrison and the ballerina. For this, I chose to use a triangle. According to Molly Bang, “We feel more scared looking at pointed shapes; we feel more secure or comforted looking at rounded shapes or curves” (Bang, 2000, pp.70-71).

Since the government constantly threatens anyone who stands out, I felt that a triangle was the most appropriate shape to represent that threat. The largest black triangle represents the Handicapper General, the prominent figure responsible for maintaining the government’s control over society and ultimately ending Harrison’s and the ballerina’s lives. The pointed shape of the triangles helps create a feeling of fear and tension, making them fitting not only for the government’s control over society but also for the red bullets coming from the Handicapper General’s gun.

Circles

            The final shape I chose to use was circles. Unlike the rectangles and triangles, the circles represent the ordinary people living in society. As I mentioned earlier, Bang explains that rounded shapes tend to make viewers feel more secure or comforted (Bang, 2000, pp.70-71). Because ordinary citizens are not presented as threatening in the same way as the government, I felt circles were a more appropriate shape to represent them.

I also felt it was a good way to show the difference between the average citizens and Harrison and the ballerina. While Harrison and the ballerina are represented by the tall vertical rectangles that appear active and energetic, the circles appear more passive and grounded. I did this to reflect the fact that most people in the story accept the government’s restrictions rather than challenge them.

Placements

The Background

            While the colors and shapes help communicate the meaning of the story, Molly Bang demonstrates throughout Picture This that an object’s placement can also affect viewers interpret an image. Because of this, I thought carefully about the placement of each element, including the background of the image, where one half is pale blue and the other half is black.

According to Molly Bang, “The upper half of a picture is a place of freedom, happiness and triumph…” (Bang, 2000, pp. 54-62). For this reason, I decided to place the pale blue portion of the image on the top half of the page. This area represents the freedom Harrison and the ballerina briefly experience after removing their handicaps.

In contrast, the black lower half represents the oppression and control imposed by the government. This fits with the second part of the quote “The bottom half of a picture feels more threatened, heavier, sadder, or more constrained…” (Bang, 2000, pp. 54-62). By separating the image into these two halves, I wanted to visually show the conflict between freedom and oppression that exists throughout the story.

Harrison and the Ballerina

            The same principle that influenced my background also helped determine the placement of Harrison and the ballerina. Since Bang associates the upper half of an image with freedom, happiness, and triumph (Bang, 2000, pp. 54-62), I placed both characters in that area.

During the dance, they no longer wear handicaps that previously limited them. Therefore, because they can express their individuality free from restrictions, I believe this makes the upper half of the image the most fitting place for them.

Something else you may have noticed was the fact that Harrison and the ballerina are center stage. Bang states that “The center of the page is the most effective ‘center of attention.’ It is the point of greatest attraction.” (Bang, 2000, pp. 62-68). Since the dance is both the climax of the story and the focus of my image, I wanted the viewer’s attention to be drawn toward them first. By placing them near the center, I hoped it would emphasize the importance of their final moments together.

Handicapper General and the Bullets

            The placement of the Handicapper General in the top half of the image is for a different reason altogether from Harrison and the ballerina. This placement takes advantage of Bang’s observation that objects placed higher on the page have “greater pictorial weight” (Bang, 2000, pp. 54-62). By placing the Handicapper General above the rest of society, I wanted to emphasize her authority and the government’s overwhelming influence over people’s lives. Even though Harrison and the ballerina briefly rise above the restrictions, the Handicapper General remains a constant presence, watching over society and enforcing its rules. This placement also helps draw attention to the source of the danger that eventually ends their moment of freedom.

The placement of the Handicapper General also influenced the placement of the bullets (the small red triangles). I wanted them to come from the Handicapper General’s direction and towards Harrison and the ballerina. By placing them this way, I wanted viewers to understand where the danger was coming from and who it was directed at.

The Viewers

            The final placement decision I made involved the viewers who are represented by the pale blue circles. By placing them in the lower half of the image, I wanted to show that the viewers of the performance were still trapped under the government’s control. Unlike Harrison and the ballerina, they had not escaped the restrictions placed on them and remained part of the oppressive society shown by the black background.

At the same time, however, I wanted the circles to create openings within the otherwise solid black area of the image. Those openings represent the brief impact Harrison and the ballerina must’ve had on those watching the performance. Although the government quickly reestablished its control quickly after their deaths, the Handicapper General ordered the musicians “…to get their handicaps back on” (Vonnegut, 1961). Despite this, I believe Harrison and the ballerina’s dance may have caused at least some viewers to question the system they were living under.

Together, I wanted the circles to represent both the people themselves and the small cracks that their moment of freedom created in the government’s control.

Conclusion

            Throughout the creation of The Last Dance, every color, shape, and placement choice was made with a specific purpose in mind. From the purple rectangles representing Harrison and the ballerina together as Emperor and Empress, and the pale blue circles representing ordinary people watching the performance and the brief impact Harrison and the ballerina had on them, to the black elements representing the government and its control over society, with red triangles representing the danger that ultimately ends their moment of freedom. Together, these elements create a visual narrative that mirrors the themes and events of the story.

By using Molly Bang’s principles from Picture This and elements of the story, I hope I was able to show the triumph and tragedy of their final dance. The brief moment of freedom they were able to achieve, as well as the inspiration I believe they may have sparked in those watching their triumph. And the tragedy of their freedom and inspiration cut short by the Handicapper General. Through my image, The Last Dance, I hoped to show not only the conflict between individuality and oppression but also the possibility that even a brief act of freedom can inspire change.
