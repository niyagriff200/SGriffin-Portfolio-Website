Consider The Checkpoint

ENG101 | Shaniya Griffin

             Dying in a game is common, especially when you’re playing for the first time. But don’t worry, there is a life-saving measure that makes sure you don’t lose all your progress: checkpoints.

            Imagine yourself in a maze. To exit the maze, you need to find four keys that will unlock the gate. These keys are guarded by some entity. You managed to grab three of the four keys, but just as you’re about to grab the last one, you are caught by the entity. Now tell me this: would you prefer to start from the very beginning and collect the keys again, or would you rather try for the last key again while already having the three keys you successfully collected?

            That is the point of checkpoints. They save players time and frustration by preventing them from doing the same thing over and over again. Instead, players can focus on the specific part they are struggling to get through, whether that is grabbing the last key or making it through a chase sequence.

            That is as far as my knowledge goes on checkpoints. But how do designers decide whether a game needs checkpoints at all? If a game does need them, when and where should they go? When should they be activated? At the beginning of the level, at the end, or both? Through this assignment, I aim to explore the who, what, when, where, and why of checkpoints.

            First things first, who invented checkpoints as we know them in video games? According to “Guinness World Records” (n.d.) 1, the first game to ever use a checkpoint was the 1984 game Pitfall 2: The Lost Caverns. I was not alive at the time, so I knew absolutely nothing about the game beforehand. However, after researching it, I learned that Pitfall 2 was developed by David Crane and released by Activision for the Atari 2600. I was unable to find direct confirmation about explaining why Crane implemented checkpoints, but Atari’s hardware was outdated, which pushed Crane to innovate. One of Crane’s most notable innovations was the Display Processor Chip. So, it can only be assumed that checkpoints were added to improve the player’s experience, despite the hardware limitations.

            Now that we know who first introduced checkpoints, the next question is what makes them so important that most games today include them. “The Role of Checkpoints” (n.d.)2 states that there are five main purposes for checkpoints: “reducing player frustration”, “maintaining game flow”, “facilitating difficulty scaling”, “encouraging exploration and risk taking”, and “supporting narrative and gameplay milestones”. While all of these purposes are important, the first two stood out to me the most.

            Returning to the maze example, needing to recollect the same keys does not make the game harder; it simply makes it frustrating and repetitive. Repeating sections that no longer serve a purpose becomes more about patience than skill. This is an important design consideration. Should the game slow the player down, or allow them to move forward and stay engaged?

            This leads to the question of when checkpoints are needed. From what I know, checkpoints are usually placed at the beginning or end of major levels, and sometimes both. This makes sense; something significant in the game is about to happen, and the designers want players to focus on that particular section. However, thinking back to the maze example, the maze itself could be considered one single level. Would breaking it into smaller sections with frequent checkpoints be the best choice?

            In the article “Why Do Video Games Use Checkpoints Instead of Free Save Systems Design Logic” (2026)3, several best practices for checkpoint design are discussed. However, three points stood out to me.

            The first is placing checkpoints after meaningful challenges. As a player, I would have to agree. I would not want to repeat a difficult section of the game after working hard to complete it.

            The second is balancing the number of checkpoints. Too many checkpoints reduce risk, while too few increase frustration. This got me thinking about my maze example again. If the maze was a total of five minutes long, and it only took about a minute to collect each key, checkpoints might not be necessary. The player could easily recover lost progress. However, if each key took 30 minutes to obtain, checkpoints would become essential. In that case, placing a checkpoint after each key would make more sense.

            The third point is respecting player effort. The article suggests avoiding checkpoints immediately before spikes in difficulty. At first, I disagreed with the idea. Wouldn’t it be better for players to focus on the part they’re struggling with? After giving it more thought, I partially agreed. In my game design classes, I was often told that designers should not hold the player’s hand throughout the entire game. Some risk is necessary. If checkpoints are placed too generously before every difficult moment, failure ends up losing meaning, and success feels less earned. That said, I still believe checkpoints should be placed shortly before major difficulty increases, to allow players a moment to rest and prepare.

            This brings up the question of where checkpoints should be placed exactly. While the previous section partially answered that question, it was more focused on when they should be placed, not where. Should checkpoints trigger immediately after completing a difficult section, or once the player enters a new, safer area?

            After reading If At First You Don’t Succeed: Helping Players Make Progress with Breaks and Checkpoints (2023)4, I found an answer. The authors discuss how players often become stuck in difficult sections and how well-placed checkpoints can help them recover. This connects back to my idea that players need time to calm down before facing increased difficulty.

            Because of this, I believe the best placement for checkpoints is in spaces players perceive as safe, such as entering a new room or defeating a major enemy. It reassures players that they will not need to repeat the same risk if they fail later.

            At this point, we know the who, what, when, and where, but why does it matter? Checkpoints may seem like a small part of game design, but they serve a larger purpose. Beyond just saving progress, I have come to realize that checkpoints signal safety, provide stopping points, and allow players to recover from tension. However, some games intentionally avoid checkpoints. Getting Over It, a game about climbing that is built around frustration and persistence, does not allow players to save progress at all. This shows that the importance of checkpoints depends on the type of experiences the designer wants to create.

            Something I realized after researching and talking to a few people is that just like in games, real life is full of moments where we wish we could respawn instead of starting over completely. Checkpoints remind us that progress does not need to be perfect to matter. What matters is having moments to pause, breathe, and try again. Whether it is studying for an exam, learning a new skill, or navigating a difficult situation, people rely on their own checkpoints. Routines, support systems, and milestones that help them keep moving forward.

            Researching checkpoints in games helped me realize how intentional these moments of rest and recovery truly are. Designers place checkpoints where players need reassurance, clarity, and a chance to rest. In the same way, recognizing our own checkpoints can help us manage frustration, stay motivated, and continue to progress through challenging moments both on screen and off.
